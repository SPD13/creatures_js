# Organ #4 — Pancreas (α-cells / Glucagon)

## Overview

Organ #4 is the **mirror image** of Organ #3. Where the insulin-producing β-cells *store* excess sugar as glycogen, Organ #4 does the opposite: it **unlocks the reserves** and puts sugar back into the bloodstream when the body needs it. In real biology this is the job of the pancreas's α-cells and the hormone **glucagon**. Together, Organs #3 and #4 form the creature's **sugar regulation loop** — one filling the tank, the other draining it.

Organ #4 has a second, less obvious role: it is the main source of the chemical that actually **makes a creature fall asleep**.

---

## In-Game Role

### Releasing stored energy

When a creature is hungry, active, or simply running low on immediate fuel, Organ #4 breaks its **Glycogen** reserves back down into **Glucose**:

- 1 × Glycogen → 6 × Glucose

That glucose then feeds every other energy-using pathway — muscle glycolysis, lung respiration, ATP synthesis. Without this organ, a Norn could build up reserves perfectly well (Organ #3 keeps storing them) but **would never be able to spend them again**. A creature whose Organ #4 has failed will look well-fed, sit on a full glycogen store, and still starve to death.

The release is large relative to the deposit: every glycogen unit becomes six units of glucose, which is why a small amount of reserves can keep a creature going for a long time once it is released.

### Heat-driven metabolism

Organ #4's own **clock rate** — the speed at which it cycles — is regulated by the creature's **Hotness** drive. A warmer body pushes the organ to work differently: the creature's rate of sugar release shifts when it is overheated, matching real-world physiology where stress hormones and body temperature interact with the pancreas.

In practice this means a hot, stressed Norn processes its reserves at a different pace from a cool, calm one. It is one of several small temperature-linked behavioural effects that give creatures a feeling of responsiveness to their environment.

### The sleep hormone source

Organ #4 is also where the creature's sleep chemistry truly *begins*. Whenever a specific brain neuron (the one tied to the sleep cycle) is active, this organ emits **Sleepase** into the bloodstream.

Sleepase is the chemical the liver (Organ #2) watches for when deciding whether a Norn can actually fall asleep. So the chain of events is:

1. The creature's brain fires the sleep neuron (tired, bored, or simply at night).
2. Organ #4 releases Sleepase in response.
3. The liver sees Sleepase in the blood and converts the sleepiness reservoir into a *felt* sleepiness drive.
4. The creature lies down and sleeps.

If Organ #4 is damaged, the creature's brain can want to sleep, but the chemical signal never arrives — and the Norn ends up **exhausted but unable to doze off**, which quickly compounds into starvation and general failure.

---

## In-Game Effects Summary

- Breaks glycogen reserves back into glucose — the reason a creature can go for a while without eating.
- Multiplies energy availability (one reserve unit → six glucose units), giving a good buffer even after a short meal.
- Its own metabolism is temperature-sensitive — hot creatures release reserves differently from cool ones.
- Produces the body's sleep-trigger chemical, so a creature with a healthy Organ #4 can actually fall asleep when tired.
- Together with Organ #3, it keeps the creature's blood-sugar balance steady: one stores, the other releases.

In short: Organ #4 is both the creature's **fuel valve** and its **sleep gate**. A Norn without a working one stays awake, hungry, and steadily weaker — even if food and reserves are there.

## Reactions, Receptors and Emitters

On the standard Starter Parent 1 genome, Organ #4 contains:

| Subsystem   | Count | Main role |
|-------------|-------|-----------|
| Reactions   | 1     | Glycogen → Glucose (reserve release). |
| Receptors   | 1     | Reads the creature's Hotness drive and uses it to modulate its own clock rate. |
| Emitters    | 1     | Emits Sleepase in response to the sleep-controlling brain neuron. |
